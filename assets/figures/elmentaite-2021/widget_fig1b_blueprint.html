Personal notes

The proportions shown here and in Figure 1b (see doi:10.1038/s41586-021-03852-1) should not be interpreted as the intestine’s exact cellular composition.

1 Adult samples

Before sequencing, the authors separated immune cells (CD45+) from all other cell types (CD45−) using magnetic sorting (MACS). The two fractions were sequenced separately and later merged. This strategy ensured that rare immune populations were captured efficiently, but it also increased their representation relative to their abundance in the tissue. As a result, immune cells appear more frequently in the dataset than they would in a histological section of the adult intestine.

2 Fetal samples

An additional step was performed for fetal tissues. After sorting, the CD45+ and CD45− fractions were recombined at a 1:1 ratio before sequencing, as stated in the Methods section: “Fetal CD45+ and CD45− fractions were combined at a 1:1 ratio.” Thus, fetal proportions do not directly reflect the native cellular composition of the developing intestine.

An analogy may help: imagine a classroom containing 90 girls and 10 boys. If you select 10 girls and 10 boys, the resulting dataset becomes 1:1 even though the classroom itself is not. Similarly, the sequencing dataset was intentionally rebalanced to better capture rare cell populations.

A potential misconception is that a 1:1 recombination should produce 50% immune cells in the final dataset. In reality, the observed proportions depend on the cellular makeup of each fraction. During the first trimester (6–11 weeks), the CD45+ fraction contains very few mature lymphocytes and is instead enriched in immature immune progenitors. Therefore, even after a 1:1 recombination, mature lymphoid populations remain rare in the final dataset, and mesenchymal cells can still dominate.

3 Pediatric samples

Unlike adult samples (obtained from deceased transplant organ donors), pediatric samples derive from endoscopic biopsies of the terminal ileum (region code TIL) taken during clinically indicated colonoscopies (8 donors, ages 4–12; see Supplementary Table 2 and Elmentaite et al., Dev. Cell 55, 771–783 (2020), doi:10.1016/j.devcel.2020.11.010). Endoscopic biopsies capture the mucosa only; deeper compartments (submucosa, muscularis propria, myenteric plexus) are not represented, which likely explains the near-absence of neural cells in the Pediatric stage. In conclusion, the Pediatric category reflects terminal-ileum mucosal biology specifically and should not be generalized to the entire intestine.
